PORTFOLIO NOTE

This is an educational case study based on the Botium Toys materials from the Google Cybersecurity Certificate. I did not test a live environment, validate configurations, or perform a formal PCI DSS, GDPR, or SOC assessment.

FocusAccess control / data confidentiality / recovery / monitoring

01Executive Summary

Botium Toys already has a useful security baseline. A firewall is filtering traffic, antivirus is monitored, data-integrity controls are in place, and the offices, storefront, and warehouse have locks, CCTV, and fire protection. The problem is that these controls are uneven. The company is stronger at protecting the perimeter and physical location than it is at deciding who should see sensitive data, protecting that data once it is stored, and recovering when something goes wrong.

The biggest concern is access. All employees can reach internally stored data, while least privilege and separation of duties are not implemented. That same weakness shows up again in the PCI DSS and SOC-related checks because customer, cardholder, and PII/SPII data are in scope. Encryption is also missing, so the confidentiality gap is not only about who can access the data, but how well the data itself is protected.

Recovery is the other major weakness. There are no backups of critical data and no disaster recovery plan. Detection is also incomplete: the firewall and antivirus provide a baseline, but there is no IDS, and legacy systems are monitored without a regular schedule or clear intervention procedure.

I kept the scenario's 8/10 risk score. My first priorities would be to restrict access to sensitive information, introduce encryption, establish backups and a recovery plan, strengthen password controls, and improve monitoring. The controls that already work should be kept and built around rather than replaced for the sake of it.

What the source material already gets right

  • Firewall rules are described as appropriately defined and active.
  • Antivirus is installed and monitored regularly.
  • Data-integrity controls are in place.
  • A 72-hour breach-notification plan exists for E.U. customers.
  • Privacy procedures are documented and enforced among relevant staff.
  • Physical security includes locks, CCTV, and fire detection/prevention.

02Scope & Method

The assessment covers the security program described in the case: employee devices, the internal network, business systems, stored data, ecommerce and inventory services, legacy systems, and the physical location. The source material also includes assets such as storefront inventory and on-premises equipment because their loss or disruption can affect business continuity.

Assessment goals

  • Identify which controls are already in place and which are missing or incomplete.
  • Connect the control gaps to the PCI DSS, GDPR, and SOC-related practices used in the exercise.
  • Turn the checklist into a practical remediation order that a stakeholder could act on.

How I scored the controls

The original exercise is mostly Yes/No. For the portfolio version I added a third status, Partially implemented, where a binary answer would hide useful context. Two examples are the password policy, which exists but is weak, and legacy-system monitoring, which exists but is not scheduled or backed by a clear intervention process.

Priorities are my analyst layer, not scores from the course material. They are there to show which gaps I would address first based on the evidence in the scenario. I did not add technical facts that were not in the supplied documents.

What is driving the risk

  • Everyone can access internally stored data; least privilege and separation of duties are missing.
  • Cardholder information is not protected with encryption in the scenario.
  • Critical data have no backup, and there is no disaster recovery plan.
  • An IDS is not installed, leaving a gap in network-level detection.
  • Password requirements are weak and are not centrally enforced.
  • Legacy systems are maintained manually, but the schedule and intervention process are unclear.

The scenario describes the impact of losing an individual asset as medium, largely because the asset picture is incomplete. The broader exposure is higher: sensitive information may be accessible too widely, and missing recovery controls could turn a security incident or system failure into a longer business interruption.

07What Stands Out

The checklist has a lot of individual rows, but the same few weaknesses keep appearing. That is useful because it means Botium Toys does not need a different fix for every failed line item. A small number of well-chosen changes would improve several areas at once.

1. Access is the common thread

The absence of least privilege and separation of duties is not an isolated administrative problem. It is the reason the PCI DSS access check fails, and it also drives the SOC-related authorization and confidentiality findings. Fixing access by role would improve several parts of the assessment at the same time.

2. Confidentiality needs more than a policy

Privacy procedures exist, but the technical controls around sensitive data are weaker. Cardholder information is not encrypted, and internal access is too broad. The written process is useful; it just needs stronger technical enforcement around it.

3. Recovery is a real blind spot

No backups and no disaster recovery plan is a risky combination. Backups without a recovery process are hard to use under pressure, and a recovery plan is not much help if the data needed to restore the business do not exist. These two controls should be treated as one recovery problem, not two unrelated checklist items.

4. Some controls exist, but they are informal

The password policy and legacy-system monitoring are both examples of partial implementation. Something is being done, but the control is not strong or repeatable enough yet. Those gaps are usually easier to close than controls that are missing entirely because there is already a starting point.

5. The baseline is worth keeping

The company is not starting from zero. Firewalling, antivirus, data integrity, privacy procedures, physical security, and breach-notification planning already provide a foundation. The remediation plan should preserve those controls while filling the access, confidentiality, recovery, and monitoring gaps.

08Recommended Order of Work

If I were handing this assessment to an IT manager, I would keep the order simple. Start with the controls that directly reduce exposure of sensitive data and improve recoverability, then move into monitoring and process maturity.

09Closing Note

Botium Toys has a workable security foundation, but the controls are not balanced. The strongest areas are perimeter, endpoint, physical security, integrity, and parts of privacy process. The weakest areas are authorization, encryption, recovery, and detection. That imbalance explains why the overall risk remains high even though several individual controls are already present.

The useful part of this case study is not the number of failed boxes. It is the way the same root causes appear across different control and compliance areas. Restricting access, protecting sensitive data, and building a real recovery capability would remove several findings at once and make the environment much easier to manage.
